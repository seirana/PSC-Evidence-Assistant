# Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis (PSC): A Universal Overview of Disease Biology, Genetics, Pathways, Clinical Features, and Treatment

**Purpose.** This document is a broad, evidence-based overview of primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) intended for education, literature-oriented retrieval, and general scientific use. It emphasizes disease mechanisms, genetic susceptibility, proteins and signaling pathways, bile-acid and metabolic biology, diagnosis, complications, and current treatment. PSC is heterogeneous, and no single mechanism explains all cases.

> **Important distinction:** PSC is not a simple monogenic disease. Most genes discussed below are **susceptibility loci or pathway-associated genes**, not single-gene causes of PSC. The disease is best understood as a multifactorial disorder in which genetic predisposition interacts with immune regulation, the gut-liver axis, bile-acid biology, cholangiocyte injury, and fibrosis.

---

## 1. What is Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis?

Primary sclerosing cholangitis is a chronic fibro-inflammatory disease of the bile ducts. It can affect intrahepatic bile ducts, extrahepatic bile ducts, or both. Persistent inflammation and tissue remodeling lead to multifocal narrowing of the biliary tree, progressive cholestasis, and, in some patients, biliary cirrhosis, portal hypertension, liver failure, and the need for liver transplantation [1].

The word **sclerosing** refers to scarring and fibrosis. **Cholangitis** refers to inflammation of the bile ducts. In PSC, the characteristic pathology is not a single obstructing lesion but a chronic pattern of bile-duct injury, inflammation, fibrosis, and alternating strictured and relatively normal or dilated segments.

PSC is rare. A population-based meta-analysis estimated a pooled prevalence of approximately **13.5 per 100,000 people**, although prevalence varies substantially between geographic regions and study populations [2]. It is more common in men than women in many cohorts and is strongly associated with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD).

More than 70% of patients with PSC have IBD in major Western cohorts. Ulcerative colitis is the most common associated form, but Crohn's disease and indeterminate colitis also occur [1]. The intestinal disease seen with PSC can differ from conventional ulcerative colitis: it may be relatively silent clinically, involve the right colon prominently, and remain histologically active despite limited symptoms.

PSC is important not only because it can progress to end-stage liver disease, but also because it markedly increases the risk of several malignancies, particularly **cholangiocarcinoma**, gallbladder cancer, and, in patients with PSC-IBD, colorectal cancer [1,3].

## 2. What Causes PSC?

The exact cause of PSC remains unknown. The strongest current model is **multifactorial causation** rather than a single initiating event.

A useful conceptual model is:

**genetic susceptibility + intestinal/immune factors + microbial and metabolite signals + bile-duct epithelial vulnerability -> chronic immune activation and cholangiocyte injury -> fibrosis and strictures -> cholestasis and progressive liver disease**

Several processes probably interact:

1. **Genetic susceptibility**, particularly within the human leukocyte antigen (HLA) region.
2. **Adaptive and innate immune dysregulation**, including T-cell signaling and inflammatory cytokine networks.
3. **The gut-liver axis**, especially in patients with IBD.
4. **Altered intestinal microbiota and microbial metabolites**.
5. **Abnormal bile-acid signaling and bile composition**.
6. **Loss of protective cholangiocyte mechanisms**, including bicarbonate secretion.
7. **Reactive or senescent cholangiocyte phenotypes** that amplify inflammation.
8. **Activation of hepatic and peribiliary fibroblasts**, which drives extracellular-matrix deposition and scarring.

AASLD guidance summarizes PSC as a disease in which multiple simultaneous mechanisms appear to contribute to initiation and progression. Strong HLA associations, additional non-HLA loci, impaired gut barrier function, intestinal dysbiosis, aberrant lymphocyte trafficking, and exposure of the liver to microbial products or metabolites all have experimental or clinical support [1].

Importantly, association does not always prove causality. Some abnormalities may initiate disease, some may accelerate it, and others may be consequences of established cholestasis.

## 3. Genetics: Susceptibility Rather Than a Single Causative Gene

PSC has one of the clearest genetic components among cholestatic liver diseases, but it is **polygenic**. Family studies show increased risk among first-degree relatives, and genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified multiple susceptibility loci [4-7].

The strongest genetic signal is within the **HLA complex on chromosome 6**, supporting an important role for antigen presentation and adaptive immunity. Both HLA class I and class II regions have been implicated. Reported PSC-associated haplotypes include combinations involving **HLA-B, HLA-DRB1, HLA-DQA1, and HLA-DQB1**, while other HLA haplotypes can be protective [8].

Large genetic studies have also identified non-HLA loci. Important examples include:

| Gene/locus | Protein or functional theme | Relevance to PSC biology |
|---|---|---|
| **HLA-B, HLA-DRB1, HLA-DQA1, HLA-DQB1 and other HLA loci** | MHC antigen-presentation proteins | Strongest genetic association; supports antigen-driven adaptive immunity |
| **IL2 / IL2RA** | Interleukin-2 and IL-2 receptor alpha (CD25) | T-cell proliferation, survival, and regulatory T-cell biology |
| **CD28** | T-cell co-stimulatory receptor | Promotes T-cell activation |
| **CTLA4** | Immune checkpoint receptor | Limits T-cell activation and contributes to immune tolerance |
| **BCL2L11** | BIM, a pro-apoptotic protein | Immune-cell survival and apoptosis |
| **MST1** | Macrophage-stimulating protein 1 pathway | Associated with immune/inflammatory regulation; a replicated non-HLA PSC locus |
| **FUT2** | Fucosyltransferase 2 | Influences mucosal glycosylation and microbial ecology |
| **UBASH3A** | T-cell signaling regulator | PSC-associated variants affect expression/splicing; linked to T-cell regulation |
| **SH2B3** | LNK adaptor protein | Immune-cell signaling and hematopoietic regulation |
| **BACH2** | Transcription factor | Immune tolerance and T-cell differentiation |
| **CLEC16A** | Immune-associated protein | Linked to several immune-mediated diseases |
| **SOCS1** | Suppressor of cytokine signaling 1 | Negative regulator of JAK/STAT cytokine signaling |
| **NFKB1** | NF-kB transcriptional regulator | Central inflammatory signaling |
| **CCL20** | Chemokine | Immune-cell recruitment; linked to inflammatory responses |
| **FOXP1** | Transcription factor | Immune-cell differentiation and regulation |
| **CCDC88B** | Immune-cell signaling protein | T-cell and inflammatory-cell functions |
| **GPR35** | G-protein-coupled receptor | Immune and intestinal biology; associated with PSC/IBD-related pathways |
| **TNFRSF14** | HVEM receptor | T-cell costimulation and immune regulation |
| **MMEL1** | Membrane metalloendopeptidase-like protein | PSC risk locus; precise mechanistic role remains under study |

The major lesson from genetics is not that one gene “causes PSC.” Instead, PSC susceptibility is enriched in genes involved in **antigen presentation, T-cell activation and tolerance, cytokine signaling, immune-cell trafficking, intestinal biology, and host-microbiome interactions** [4-8].

The 2013 Immunochip study identified multiple genome-wide significant non-HLA loci, and a later large GWAS added further loci and showed that PSC shares some genetic architecture with ulcerative colitis while remaining genetically distinct from both ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease [5,6]. Thus, PSC and IBD overlap biologically without being the same disease.

## 4. Immune Dysregulation and Antigen Presentation

The strong HLA signal suggests that antigen presentation is central to disease susceptibility. HLA proteins display peptide antigens to T cells. In genetically susceptible individuals, the combination of available antigens, HLA structure, co-stimulatory signals, and immune-regulatory pathways may favor abnormal or persistent T-cell responses.

Several PSC-associated genes converge on **T-cell biology**:

- **IL-2** promotes T-cell proliferation and supports regulatory T-cell homeostasis.
- **IL2RA (CD25)** is highly expressed on regulatory T cells and activated T cells.
- **CD28** supplies a key co-stimulatory signal necessary for full T-cell activation.
- **CTLA4** opposes CD28 signaling and helps restrain T-cell activation.
- **BACH2**, **UBASH3A**, **SH2B3**, and **SOCS1** influence immune activation, tolerance, or cytokine signaling.

Portal inflammatory infiltrates in PSC contain T cells as well as macrophages, natural killer cells, B cells, and other immune populations. The relative contribution of each cell type can vary by disease stage and tissue compartment.

Inflammatory signaling pathways include **NF-kB**, cytokines such as TNF, IL-1 family members, IL-6, and T-cell-related pathways including Th1/Th17 responses. However, it is difficult to separate primary pathogenic immune responses from secondary inflammation caused by bile-duct obstruction, cholestasis, bacterial cholangitis, and tissue injury.

---

## 5. The Gut-Liver Axis

The close relationship between PSC and IBD is one of the central clues to pathogenesis.

Blood from the intestine reaches the liver through the portal vein. Therefore, the liver is continuously exposed to intestinally derived nutrients, microbial molecules, metabolites, and immune signals. When the intestinal barrier, microbiome, or mucosal immune system changes, the liver is one of the first organs exposed to those changes.

### 5.1 Aberrant lymphocyte homing

A major hypothesis is that lymphocytes activated in the gut can later migrate to the liver.

Important molecules include:

- **alpha4beta7 integrin** on gut-primed lymphocytes
- **MAdCAM-1**, an endothelial adhesion molecule
- **CCR9**, a chemokine receptor on subsets of gut-homing lymphocytes
- **CCL25**, the ligand for CCR9
- **VAP-1**, vascular adhesion protein-1

In the intestine, alpha4beta7 helps lymphocytes bind MAdCAM-1, while CCR9 responds to CCL25. In PSC, ectopic or increased expression of gut-associated adhesion and chemokine systems has been demonstrated in hepatic tissue, supporting the idea that gut-primed lymphocytes can be recruited to the liver [9-11].

This model is biologically attractive, but it should not be treated as a complete explanation. Recent work suggests that some “gut-homing” features are also seen in other chronic liver diseases, so the pathway may contribute to PSC without being uniquely causal [11].

### 5.2 Intestinal barrier and microbial products

Another hypothesis is the **leaky-gut model**. Increased intestinal permeability may permit bacterial products or metabolites to enter portal circulation, activating:

- Kupffer cells and other macrophages
- sinusoidal endothelial cells
- cholangiocytes
- dendritic cells
- recruited lymphocytes

Toll-like receptors and other pattern-recognition pathways can then amplify inflammatory responses.

---

## 6. Microbiome and Microbial Metabolites

Patients with PSC often have an intestinal microbiome that differs from healthy controls and from IBD patients without PSC. Reported changes include altered microbial diversity and changes in organisms involved in bile-acid transformation and metabolite production [12,13].

No single microorganism has been proven to be the universal cause of PSC. Rather, dysbiosis may alter the chemical environment to which the intestine and liver are exposed.

Potential mechanisms include:

1. Increased or altered microbial products reaching the portal circulation.
2. Changes in primary-to-secondary bile-acid conversion.
3. Changes in short-chain fatty acids and other immunoregulatory metabolites.
4. Altered mucosal barrier integrity.
5. Changes in antigen exposure and lymphocyte activation.
6. Interactions between host genotype, such as **FUT2**, and microbial ecology.

Microbiome differences may therefore act both upstream and downstream of inflammation. Antibiotic studies, including studies of oral vancomycin, support biological interest in this area, but clinical evidence is still insufficient to establish routine antibiotic therapy as disease-modifying treatment for PSC [1,14].

## 7. Bile-Acid Metabolism: A Major Metabolic Axis in PSC

PSC is not primarily an inherited metabolic disorder, but **bile-acid metabolism and signaling are central to disease biology**.

Bile acids are synthesized from cholesterol in hepatocytes. A key rate-limiting enzyme is **cholesterol 7alpha-hydroxylase (CYP7A1)**. Primary bile acids are secreted into bile, pass through the biliary tree into the intestine, assist lipid absorption, and are then largely reabsorbed in the terminal ileum.

The enterohepatic circulation can be simplified as:

**cholesterol -> hepatic primary bile-acid synthesis -> biliary secretion -> intestine -> microbial transformation -> ileal reabsorption -> portal return to liver**

### 7.1 FXR-FGF19-FGFR4-CYP7A1 signaling

The nuclear receptor **FXR**, encoded by **NR1H4**, is a key bile-acid sensor.

When bile acids activate intestinal FXR:

1. intestinal cells increase production of **FGF19**;
2. FGF19 enters portal blood;
3. FGF19 binds the hepatic **FGFR4/beta-Klotho** receptor complex;
4. signaling suppresses **CYP7A1**;
5. hepatic bile-acid synthesis decreases.

This is a negative-feedback loop that helps control the size and composition of the bile-acid pool [12,13].

FXR also influences inflammatory, metabolic, and epithelial-barrier pathways. Abnormalities in the FXR/FGF19 axis have been described in PSC, although findings can reflect compensatory responses to chronic cholestasis rather than a single initiating defect.

### 7.2 TGR5 / GPBAR1

**TGR5**, also called **GPBAR1**, is a membrane bile-acid receptor expressed on several cell types, including cholangiocytes and immune cells.

In cholangiocytes, TGR5 signaling is linked to chloride and bicarbonate secretion and epithelial protection. In immune cells, TGR5 can reduce inflammatory signaling, including NF-kB-dependent pathways [12,13].

### 7.3 The biliary bicarbonate umbrella

Cholangiocytes need protection from the detergent effects of bile acids. One protective concept is the **biliary bicarbonate umbrella**, which creates a bicarbonate-rich environment at the apical surface of biliary epithelial cells.

Important proteins include:

- **CFTR** — chloride channel involved in biliary secretion
- **AE2 / SLC4A2** — chloride/bicarbonate exchanger
- **TGR5 / GPBAR1** — bile-acid receptor contributing to secretory and protective signaling

Reduced effectiveness of these mechanisms can make cholangiocytes more vulnerable to bile-acid-induced injury [12,15].

### 7.4 Microbial conversion of bile acids

Gut bacteria deconjugate and chemically transform primary bile acids into secondary bile acids such as deoxycholic acid and lithocholic acid. These molecules differ in hydrophobicity and in their ability to activate receptors such as FXR, TGR5, VDR, and other nuclear receptors.

This creates an important feedback loop:

**microbiome -> bile-acid composition -> receptor signaling -> intestinal barrier + immune responses + hepatic metabolism**

and, in the opposite direction:

**bile-acid composition -> microbial ecological pressure**

This bidirectional relationship is one reason the microbiome and bile-acid system are difficult to study independently in PSC.

## 8. Cholangiocytes: More Than Passive Targets

Cholangiocytes are the epithelial cells lining the bile ducts. In PSC they are not simply passive victims of inflammation.

When injured or exposed to inflammatory and microbial signals, cholangiocytes can enter a **reactive phenotype**. They can produce cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and extracellular-matrix-associated signals that recruit immune cells and activate nearby fibroblastic cells.

Cholangiocyte dysfunction can include:

- impaired bicarbonate secretion;
- altered bile-acid receptor signaling;
- increased responsiveness to pathogen-associated molecules;
- oxidative and cellular stress;
- cellular senescence;
- secretion of inflammatory and pro-fibrotic mediators.

Senescent cholangiocytes may develop a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), producing mediators that perpetuate inflammation and tissue remodeling. This creates the possibility of a self-sustaining loop in which initial injury leads to a cholangiocyte state that continues to promote inflammation and fibrosis.

---

## 9. Fibrogenesis and Bile-Duct Remodeling

The defining structural outcome of PSC is progressive periductal fibrosis.

Repeated epithelial injury and inflammation activate:

- portal fibroblasts,
- hepatic stellate cells,
- myofibroblasts,
- macrophage-derived signaling pathways,
- extracellular-matrix remodeling systems.

**TGF-beta signaling** is a major pro-fibrotic pathway in many liver diseases and contributes to fibroblast activation and collagen deposition. Other inflammatory pathways, oxidative stress signals, integrins, and matrix-remodeling enzymes also participate.

Histologically, PSC can show periductal fibrosis and fibro-obliterative bile-duct lesions. The classic “onion-skin” pattern of concentric periductal fibrosis is well known but is not present in every biopsy and is not by itself required for diagnosis [1].

Progressive fibrosis narrows bile ducts. Narrowing causes impaired bile flow, which increases local bile pressure and cholestatic injury. Thus, once established, PSC can develop a reinforcing cycle:

**inflammation -> fibrosis -> obstruction -> cholestasis -> further epithelial injury and inflammation**

## 10. Major Biological Pathways in PSC

The following pathways provide a useful systems-level view of PSC:

### Antigen presentation
**HLA class I and II -> T-cell recognition -> adaptive immune activation**

### IL-2 / T-cell regulation
**IL-2 -> IL2RA/CD25 -> T-cell survival and regulatory T-cell biology**

### T-cell co-stimulation and tolerance
**CD28 <-> CTLA4 -> balance between activation and immune restraint**

### Gut-liver lymphocyte trafficking
**CCL25 -> CCR9**  
**MAdCAM-1 -> alpha4beta7 integrin**  
**VAP-1 -> endothelial activation and adhesion**

### Bile-acid feedback
**FXR/NR1H4 -> FGF19 -> FGFR4/beta-Klotho -> suppression of CYP7A1**

### Cholangiocyte protection
**TGR5/GPBAR1 -> CFTR/AE2-dependent secretory processes -> bicarbonate-rich epithelial protection**

### Inflammatory signaling
**microbial products / cellular stress -> pattern-recognition pathways -> NF-kB and cytokine production**

### Inflammasome-related signaling
Bile acids and microbial signals can influence **NLRP3 inflammasome** activity in immune cells, linking metabolic sensing and inflammation [13].

### Fibrogenesis
**chronic injury + inflammatory mediators -> fibroblast/stellate-cell activation -> TGF-beta and extracellular-matrix deposition -> periductal fibrosis**

These pathways overlap. For example, bile acids are simultaneously metabolic molecules, detergents, receptor ligands, and immune modulators.

---

## 11. Clinical Presentation

PSC can be discovered incidentally because of abnormal liver tests, particularly a cholestatic pattern with elevated alkaline phosphatase (ALP). Other patients present with symptoms.

Possible features include:

- fatigue;
- pruritus;
- jaundice;
- abdominal discomfort;
- fever and chills during bacterial cholangitis;
- weight loss;
- biochemical cholestasis;
- complications of portal hypertension in advanced disease.

PSC can fluctuate. Liver-test abnormalities may improve or worsen over time and do not perfectly measure fibrosis or long-term disease activity.

---

## 12. Diagnosis

The diagnosis is based on the clinical context, cholestatic liver tests, exclusion of competing causes, and characteristic imaging.

**MRI with MR cholangiopancreatography (MRI/MRCP)** is the preferred non-invasive diagnostic imaging method. Typical large-duct PSC shows multifocal strictures involving intrahepatic and/or extrahepatic bile ducts, often alternating with relatively preserved or dilated segments [1].

Important principles from AASLD guidance include:

- ERCP should generally **not** be used simply to establish the diagnosis when adequate MRCP is available.
- Serum **IgG4** should be measured to help exclude IgG4-related sclerosing cholangitis.
- Liver biopsy is useful when **small-duct PSC** is suspected despite a normal cholangiogram or when autoimmune-hepatitis overlap is being considered.
- Patients newly diagnosed with PSC who do not already have known IBD should undergo ileocolonoscopy with biopsies because colitis may be asymptomatic [1].

No single blood biomarker is specific enough to diagnose PSC.

## 13. Major Complications

PSC is clinically important because disease progression and complications can involve several organ systems.

### Cholestasis and biliary obstruction

Fibrotic strictures can obstruct bile flow. A **relevant stricture** may produce clinical or biochemical obstruction and can predispose to bacterial cholangitis.

### Bacterial cholangitis

Obstructed bile ducts can become infected. Acute cholangitis may present with fever, pain, jaundice, or systemic illness and requires prompt medical treatment.

### Progressive fibrosis and portal hypertension

Chronic biliary injury can progress to cirrhosis and portal hypertension, resulting in varices, ascites, splenomegaly, thrombocytopenia, and hepatic decompensation.

### Cholangiocarcinoma

PSC is a major risk factor for cholangiocarcinoma. Estimates vary among cohorts, but lifetime prevalence in PSC is often reported in the range of roughly 6-13% [3]. New or worsening cholestasis, weight loss, dominant/relevant strictures, increasing CA 19-9, or suspicious imaging may require focused evaluation.

### Gallbladder cancer

Gallbladder polyps and masses require careful surveillance because malignant potential is greater in PSC than in the general population.

### Colorectal cancer

PSC-IBD carries a substantially increased risk of colorectal dysplasia and cancer compared with IBD alone. Regular colonoscopic surveillance is therefore an important component of care [1].

### Bone and nutritional complications

Long-standing cholestatic liver disease can contribute to metabolic bone disease and deficiencies of fat-soluble vitamins, especially in advanced cholestasis.

## 14. Treatment: What Can Be Done Today?

As of 2026, **there is no approved medical therapy proven to stop or reverse PSC progression**. Current management focuses on risk stratification, surveillance, treatment of complications, symptom control, selected use of ursodeoxycholic acid, management of associated IBD, endoscopic treatment when needed, clinical trials, and liver transplantation [1,16-18].

### 14.1 Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA)

UDCA is a hydrophilic bile acid and the most extensively studied medication in PSC.

AASLD states that **UDCA 13-23 mg/kg/day can be considered** in patients with persistently elevated ALP or GGT who are not participating in a clinical trial, and can be continued if it is well tolerated and produces meaningful biochemical and/or symptomatic improvement within approximately 12 months [1].

However, UDCA has not been proven to improve transplant-free survival in PSC. **High-dose UDCA (approximately 28 mg/kg/day or more) should be avoided** because high-dose trials raised safety concerns [1].

### 14.2 Antibiotics and oral vancomycin

Antibiotics are appropriate for **bacterial cholangitis**.

Oral vancomycin has generated considerable interest because of the gut-liver and microbiome hypotheses. Small trials and observational studies have reported biochemical or clinical signals, but evidence remains insufficient for routine use of oral vancomycin as a disease-modifying PSC treatment. AASLD therefore does not recommend it routinely for PSC itself [1,14].

### 14.3 Endoscopic therapy

ERCP is used when there is a therapeutic or diagnostic indication, not as routine screening.

Possible indications include:

- bacterial cholangitis not adequately responding to antibiotics;
- progressive bile-duct dilation;
- clinically important strictures;
- unexplained worsening liver tests;
- worsening pruritus;
- concern for cholangiocarcinoma.

Balloon dilation and, in selected cases, temporary stenting may improve drainage. Suspicious strictures may require brush cytology and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) as part of the evaluation for malignancy [1].

### 14.4 Management of IBD

IBD should be treated according to appropriate gastroenterology guidelines. Control of bowel inflammation is important for intestinal health, but successful IBD therapy does not automatically halt PSC progression.

### 14.5 Symptom management

Pruritus can be a major problem. Treatment is individualized and may include bile-acid-binding resins and other anti-pruritic strategies used in cholestatic liver disease. Fatigue is common but has no single PSC-specific therapy.

### 14.6 Liver transplantation

Liver transplantation remains the definitive treatment for decompensated or end-stage PSC and can also be considered in selected patients with severe recurrent bacterial cholangitis or carefully selected PSC-associated perihilar cholangiocarcinoma under specialized protocols [1].

PSC can recur after liver transplantation, but transplantation still provides major survival benefit for appropriate candidates.

## 15. Investigational and Emerging Therapeutic Strategies

Drug development increasingly reflects the biological pathways described above.

### Bile-acid and metabolic approaches

- **norursodeoxycholic acid (norUDCA / norucholic acid)** alters bile-acid handling and has anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic rationale. Phase III study results reported in 2026 showed improvement in the trial's primary biochemical/histologic endpoint, but it is not yet an approved PSC disease-modifying therapy [18].
- **FXR agonists** attempt to regulate bile-acid synthesis, transport, inflammation, and metabolic signaling.
- **PPAR agonists** target metabolic and inflammatory transcriptional programs.
- **IBAT/ASBT inhibitors** modify enterohepatic bile-acid circulation and are also being explored for cholestatic pruritus.
- **FGF19-related approaches** target bile-acid feedback signaling.

### Immune and inflammatory approaches

Candidate strategies include blocking inflammatory cytokines, immune-cell recruitment, chemokine pathways, or adhesion molecules. Examples investigated experimentally or clinically include pathways involving **VAP-1**, **CCL24**, and other inflammatory/fibrotic mediators.

### Anti-fibrotic approaches

Because fibrosis determines many clinical outcomes, anti-fibrotic strategies aim to interrupt fibroblast activation, extracellular-matrix deposition, or signaling between cholangiocytes, macrophages, and stromal cells.

### Microbiome-directed approaches

Research includes:

- targeted antibiotics;
- bacteriophage strategies;
- engineered probiotics;
- fecal microbiota transplantation;
- microbial-metabolite manipulation.

These approaches remain investigational. The microbiome is a promising target but is complex, and changing microbiota does not necessarily translate into durable modification of PSC progression.

## 16. A Systems-Level View of PSC

PSC can be understood as a network rather than a linear disease.

### Layer 1: genetic susceptibility
HLA and non-HLA variants alter thresholds for immune activation, tolerance, mucosal biology, and host-microbiome interaction.

### Layer 2: intestinal environment
IBD, barrier dysfunction, microbiome composition, bile-acid transformation, and microbial metabolites modify immune exposure.

### Layer 3: immune trafficking and activation
Gut-primed lymphocytes, macrophages, endothelial adhesion systems, chemokines, and cytokines promote hepatic and peribiliary inflammation.

### Layer 4: cholangiocyte response
Cholangiocytes sense bile acids, microbial products, cytokines, and mechanical stress. Protective secretory mechanisms may fail, while reactive and senescent programs can amplify injury.

### Layer 5: fibrosis
Portal fibroblasts and hepatic stellate cells respond to chronic injury by depositing extracellular matrix around ducts.

### Layer 6: structural and metabolic consequences
Strictures impair bile flow, causing cholestasis and repeated tissue injury.

### Layer 7: clinical outcomes
Cirrhosis, portal hypertension, bacterial cholangitis, malignancy, and liver transplantation can result.

This framework explains why a drug aimed at only one pathway may improve a biomarker such as ALP without necessarily changing every downstream clinical outcome.

---

## 17. Key Takeaways

- PSC is a **chronic immune-associated fibro-inflammatory cholangiopathy** characterized by progressive bile-duct injury and fibrosis.
- The **cause is not known** and is almost certainly multifactorial.
- **HLA loci** provide the strongest genetic association, while non-HLA genes implicate T-cell signaling, immune tolerance, cytokine regulation, intestinal biology, and host-microbiome interactions.
- PSC and IBD are closely linked, but PSC is not simply a liver complication of ordinary ulcerative colitis.
- The **gut-liver axis**, microbial dysbiosis, lymphocyte trafficking, and microbial metabolites are major mechanistic areas.
- **Bile acids are both metabolic molecules and signaling molecules**. FXR/FGF19 and TGR5-related biology connect metabolism, immunity, epithelial protection, and microbiome activity.
- Cholangiocytes actively participate in disease through inflammatory, secretory, stress, and senescence programs.
- Fibrosis creates a feed-forward cycle of bile-duct narrowing, cholestasis, and additional injury.
- There is currently **no approved disease-modifying pharmacologic therapy** for PSC.
- Management therefore combines surveillance, treatment of strictures and infections, management of IBD and symptoms, selective UDCA use, clinical-trial participation, and liver transplantation when appropriate.
- The most promising future treatments are likely to emerge from combinations of **immune, metabolic, bile-acid, microbiome, cholangiocyte, and anti-fibrotic biology**, rather than from a single universal target.
